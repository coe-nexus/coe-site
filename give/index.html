Why Most Approaches Miss

You can't build confidence on a foundation of "I'm not enough."

Most programs try to motivate teens toward who they "should" become. The 7 Teachings starts somewhere quieter and far more powerful: helping a teen accept the person they already are. That acceptance is the door. Once a teen stops fighting themselves, confidence and courage stop being things they have to manufacture — they become things that show up on their own.

This isn't therapy, and no one gets "fixed." Teens are handed a structured framework, a mentor who treats them as capable, and a series of small challenges that surface abilities they never knew they had.

Proof It Works

They couldn't see it. Neither could anyone else.

No therapy. No one got "fixed." Both teens were simply handed a structured framework for discovering capabilities that were already present — but invisible to them.

Tap a name to read the whole story.

Elly was jobless and nearly out of money, communicating through a broken laptop on WhatsApp because she couldn't afford a phone. She was looking for work as a minimum-wage nanny, and weeks from being evicted for unpaid rent.

Within 10 weeks of structured mentorship through the 7 Teachings, she directed and produced her first documentary. Shot in 6K. Hired her own videographers. Rented equipment she'd never touched. Edited on software she'd never opened.

None of this was on her radar. Not dormant. Not buried. Nonexistent. She had never once imagined herself behind a camera.

What happened in between was a sequence of small challenges designed to surface capabilities she didn't know she had — editing in Descript, working with scripts, locations, lighting. Each one slightly past the edge of what she believed she could do. Each one proving the belief wrong.

Within six months she had built a coded media app for kids using Claude Code, found her own clients beyond nanny work, and was selected for a Brazilian voice-talent award.

"If not for Izzy and the 7 Teachings, I wouldn't have found the confidence to try programming and build a product on my own."

Dimash was depressed, copy-pasting his way through classes in Kazakhstan. Addicted to video games. Surrounded by classmates going nowhere. His parents were divorced, both relationships strained. He didn't believe he could get into any school beyond a small local community college — let alone earn a scholarship abroad. A full ride wasn't a stretch goal. It was unthinkable.

Rather than treating him as a student, Izzy brought him in as a working partner. Real client work for American tech companies. Negotiation rooms where he had to hold his own against professionals three times his age.

Within six months he was building his own software products. Not school projects — actual tools solving real problems for real users.

When it came time for university applications, he refused help with his essays. He told his mother: "No one can say it better about me than I can myself."

SAT: 1540. He applied to MIT, KAIST, and top programs in Hong Kong and Singapore — every essay written from genuine self-knowledge, not performance.

Twelve months after his last session with Izzy, he was accepted on a full scholarship to CUHK's financial-engineering program, one of the strongest in Asia. He's there now, on his way to becoming a quantitative trader.

"Without Izzy, he would have been lost — he would never have uncovered his true talent or seen himself for what he is capable of." — his mother
